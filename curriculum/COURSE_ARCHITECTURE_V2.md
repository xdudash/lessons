# SlovakGo — Course Architecture

## ACTIVE MODEL

SlovakGo is a complete Slovak CEFR A1–C2 course.

**Lesson count is an output of curriculum design, never a predefined input.**

Retired quotas:

- 10,000 total lessons;
- 1,000 A1 lessons;
- 40 lessons per section;
- any requirement to fill numerical slots.

These may exist in historical audits but must never drive generation.

The same principle applies to A1: the current lesson count is a planning estimate, not a quota.

## CEFR progression

### A1 — Forms and fundamentals
Basic high-frequency structures, core vocabulary, simple interaction, immediate practical communication.

### A2 — Systems and controlled expansion
Broader grammatical systems, routine narration, controlled combinations, wider everyday functions.

### B1 — Connections and independent communication
Connected discourse, explanations, experiences, plans, reasons, problem solving and increasing independence.

### B2 — Composition and complex communication
Complex syntax, argumentation, register awareness, composition and flexible interaction.

### C1 — Choice and precision
Fine-grained lexical/grammatical choice, discourse organization, register, implication and precision.

### C2 — Control and nuance
Near-complete flexibility, stylistic control, subtle distinctions, idiomaticity, inference and adaptation.

## Mastery model

Curriculum progression is:

**NEW → STABILIZE → CONTRAST → TRANSFER → INTEGRATE → MASTERY**

A target may move through these stages more than once and may require different amounts of practice.

These are functions, not fixed lesson numbers.

### A1 unit scope

#### 1. Sounds, reading and basic word structure — 6
1. Slovak alphabet and letter-to-sound correspondence
2. Vowels, consonants and reading simple words
3. Syllabic `r, ŕ, l, ĺ`
4. Soft pronunciation: `ď, ť, ň`, `de/te/ne`, `di/ti/ni`
5. Length, short/long vowels, diphthongs and rhythm
6. Stress, intonation and reading short messages

#### 2. Me, you, he/she: introductions and personal data — 7
1. Who am I? `byť`, name, country and language
2. `ja, ty, on, ona, ono`
3. `my, vy, oni/ony`
4. Greetings and leave-taking
5. Asking about a person
6. Name, age, nationality, address and phone number
7. Real introduction: dialogue, form and short message

#### 3. People, family and describing people — 6
1. People and relationships
2. Grammatical gender of nouns
3. Plural and basic agreement
4. Adjectives and describing people
5. Family, age, profession and nationality
6. Describing a person in speech and a short text

#### 4. Things, possession and my world — 6
1. What is this? Who is this?
2. Noun gender and forms in real use
3. Possessives: `môj, tvoj, jeho, jej...`
4. Possession and relationships between things
5. What I have / do not have
6. Describing personal things and surroundings

#### 5. Home, rooms and location of things — 6
1. Home and rooms
2. Furniture and objects
3. Where things are
4. Prepositions of place
5. Describing a room/apartment
6. Practical situation: explaining where something is

#### 6. Actions and my everyday day — 7
1. Core everyday actions
2. Infinitive and present tense
3. Main conjugation patterns
4. `byť`, `mať` and frequent irregular verbs
5. Reflexive verbs and `sa`
6. Negation
7. My day: sequence of actions, short account and dialogue

#### 7. Numbers, quantity, time and dates — 6
1. Numbers
2. Age, phone numbers, addresses and prices
3. Clock time
4. Days of the week and parts of the day
5. Dates, months and years
6. Schedules and arrangements

#### 8. City, place and movement — 7
1. Places in the city
2. Where I am
3. Where I am going / travelling
4. Prepositions and case forms for space
5. Directions
6. Asking for and explaining a route
7. Real city situation: route, transport and clarification

#### 9. Food, drinks and café — 6
1. Food and drinks
2. Likes and dislikes
3. Quantity and portions
4. Menu language
5. Ordering in a café/restaurant
6. Full situation: choose, order, clarify and pay

#### 10. Shopping, money, clothing and services — 6
1. Shop and goods
2. Price and quantity
3. Size, colour and characteristics
4. Clothing
5. Requests, choices and payment
6. Shop + service + problem/clarification

#### 11. Study, work and everyday responsibilities — 5
1. Study and study actions
2. Work and professions
3. Where I work/study and what I do
4. Schedules and responsibilities
5. Short account of study/work + questions to another person

#### 12. Free time, interests, plans and social communication — 5
1. Hobbies and free time
2. Likes and dislikes
3. Suggestions and invitations
4. Simple plans and arrangements
5. Social dialogue: propose, accept/refuse and agree on a plan

#### 13. Body, health, problems and help — 4
1. Body parts and basic state of health
2. Simple health problems
3. Pharmacy/doctor/help
4. Explain a problem, understand advice and ask for repetition/clarification

#### 14. A1 integration: real life — 5
1. Me and my life: personal data, family, home and day
2. In the city: place, transport, directions and time
3. Shopping and services: goods, price, quantity, request and problem
4. Social life: introduction, café, invitation, plans and messages
5. A1 survival simulation combining comprehension, questions, answers, repair, information transfer, short writing and practical problem solving

### A1 cross-cutting competencies

The following are **not separate artificial units**. They must be distributed through the thematic curriculum:

- listening and reading reception;
- spoken production;
- spoken interaction;
- written production;
- online interaction;
- basic mediation;
- communication repair and clarification;
- sociolinguistic appropriateness;
- politeness and formal/informal interaction;
- discourse organization and basic connectors;
- pronunciation and intelligibility;
- review, transfer and mastery.

A1 must therefore not end up as a sequence of vocabulary topics with grammar attached. Each unit is a vehicle for coordinated development of linguistic, communicative, pragmatic and sociolinguistic competence.

## Unit design

Every unit must define:

1. learning outcomes;
2. linguistic targets;
3. communicative functions;
4. vocabulary/expressions;
5. prerequisites;
6. review dependencies;
7. transfer opportunities;
8. integration requirements;
9. mastery gate.

Only after this definition is the number of lessons chosen.

## Lesson-count optimization rule

The number of lessons must be derived from the **atomic learning targets**, not from a desired total.

For each unit:

1. enumerate atomic targets;
2. identify prerequisites and dependencies;
3. group targets that can be learned efficiently together;
4. separate targets when cognitive load, contrast, practice or mastery evidence requires it;
5. place deliberate review and transfer;
6. verify that the resulting lessons provide sufficient mastery evidence;
7. remove any lesson whose purpose can be achieved properly elsewhere;
8. add lessons whenever compression would weaken learning.

The optimization objective is:

> **the smallest pedagogically sufficient curriculum that achieves reliable A1 mastery — not the smallest possible number of lessons.**

Therefore neither 82 nor any other number is sacred. A final A1 lesson count may differ from the current working estimate after detailed curriculum analysis.

## Section design

Every section must define:

1. learning outcomes;
2. linguistic targets;
3. communicative functions;
4. vocabulary/expressions;
5. prerequisites;
6. review dependencies;
7. transfer opportunities;
8. integration requirements;
9. mastery gate.

Only after this definition is the number of lessons chosen.

## Production batch

Production uses **3 coherent consecutive sections per batch**.

Each selected section normally receives **3–6 lessons** in the initial batch when production is required.

Three is the minimum starting depth. Six is the maximum for that batch, not a quota. If evidence later shows that more practice is needed, a later batch may extend the section.

This production-batch constraint does **not** override the curriculum optimization rule: it governs production batches, not the final number of lessons required by the course.

## Complete lesson architecture

A strong lesson normally follows:

**TARGET → THEORY → EXAMPLES → VOCABULARY → CONTROLLED PRACTICE → CONTRAST → CONTEXT → PRODUCTION → COMMUNICATION → REAL LIFE → MASTERY EVIDENCE**

The exact order may vary when pedagogy requires it.

## Gold standard

The canonical `A1-S01-L01` is the quality reference. It demonstrates:

- complete lesson envelope;
- 2 theory screens;
- 6 vocabulary items;
- 16 exercises;
- varied exercise mechanics;
- contextual meaning;
- grammar/vocabulary/reading/dialogue/writing practice;
- natural phrases;
- multi-step final situation;
- localization;
- completion/result structure.

The lesson's exact content must not be copied.

## Functional exposure vs mastery

A useful expression may contain grammar that is formally taught later. That is allowed as functional exposure.

Do not claim mastery of a later grammatical system merely because a phrase containing it appeared earlier.

## Existing content

Existing lessons are evidence and source material. They must be mapped to current targets and audited for quality.

A structural parse does not prove pedagogical quality.

## Vocabulary ownership

Vocabulary repetition is valid when it serves review, transfer or mastery. Accidental duplication is not.

Use target ownership to determine whether a word/expression is:

- NEW;
- REVIEW;
- TRANSFER;
- MASTERY.

Frequency alone does not prove mastery.

## Section completion gate

Do not mark a section complete until:

- all declared outcomes are covered;
- prerequisites are respected;
- core vocabulary/functions are usable;
- review and transfer are present where required;
- integrated use is demonstrated;
- mastery evidence is sufficient.

## No filler

If one lesson can achieve the defined outcome properly, do not create three merely because three is the minimum production starting point. The minimum applies only when multiple lessons are pedagogically required.

Every lesson must have a distinct learning purpose.
